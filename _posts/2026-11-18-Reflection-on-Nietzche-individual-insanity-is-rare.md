This afternoon, I was reading Muzafer Sherif’s classic psychology paper on the autokinetic effect, and it left me thinking about just how much pull collective opinion has over individual judgment.

In Sherif’s experiment, participants were placed in a completely dark room and they were then asked to estimate how far a tiny point of light moved. In reality, the light never moved at all—the perceived motion was actually just an optical illusion. When tested alone, each person developed their own individual baseline estimate. Yet when placed into small groups and asked to state their estimates out loud, something striking happened: their answers gradually converged toward a shared group norm. Even more telling, when participants were later tested alone again, they continued to rely on the group's estimate rather than returning to their original personal judgment.

Reading that brought to mind one of Friedrich Nietzsche’s sharpest aphorisms from *Beyond Good and Evil*:

> "Insanity in individuals is something rare - but in groups, parties, nations and epochs, it is the rule."

Sherif’s experiment offers a quiet, everyday window into why Nietzsche’s observation rings so true. When reality is ambiguous—whether we are staring at a pinpoint of light in a dark room or navigating complex social and intellectual questions—standing alone feels uncomfortable. Most of us carry an instinctive hesitation to be the outlier, so we subtly adjust our perception to match the room. Over time, that convergence stops feeling like compromise and starts feeling like objective truth.

What makes this both fascinating and cautionary is that collective error does not require malicious intent. A group of reasonable, thoughtful individuals can still drift toward a distorted consensus simply because each person is anchoring their judgment to everyone else.

For me, the takeaway is simple: whenever I find myself agreeing effortlessly with the crowd, it is worth pausing to ask whether I actually see the light moving—or whether I am just echoing the room.
